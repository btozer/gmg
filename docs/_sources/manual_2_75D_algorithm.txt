============================================================
**A3 Rasmussen and Pedersen (1979) 2.75D end corrections**
============================================================

The algorithms described in Sections :ref:`A1 <manual_gravity_algorithm>` and
:ref:`A2 <magnetic_algorithm>` treat a model layer as a polygon of **infinite** extent orthogonal to the
model cross section. A real geological body is of course of finite length along strike, and an infinite body
always produces a larger anomaly than a finite one of the same cross section. Modelling a finite body as an
infinite one therefore biases the fitted density and susceptibility contrasts **low**.

gmg corrects for this using the end corrections of Rasmussen and Pedersen (1979) :cite:`rasmussen1979`, who
showed that a single fundamental integral serves both the gravity and the magnetic case, and that the two
dimensional formulae of Talwani et al. (1959) :cite:`talwani1959` and Talwani and Heirtzler (1964)
:cite:`talwani1964` need only a modest change to represent a body whose tails in the strike direction have
been cut off. This is the same formulation used by commercial packages such as GM-SYS. The particular form
of the expressions implemented here follows Zunino et al. (2022) :cite:`zunino2022`.

2.5D and 2.75D
--------------

The extent of a layer in the strike direction is given by two signed distances measured from the plane of
the model section:

* :math:`y_{1}`, the extent on the negative side
* :math:`y_{2}`, the extent on the positive side

Two cases are distinguished in the literature:

* **2.5D**, where the body extends equally either side of the section, i.e. :math:`y_{1} = -y_{2}`
* **2.75D**, where the two sides differ, i.e. :math:`y_{1} \neq -y_{2}`

gmg implements the general 2.75D case, of which 2.5D is simply the symmetric special case. Setting an
asymmetric pair of extents is useful where a body is known to terminate closer to the profile on one side
than the other.

As :math:`y_{1} \rightarrow -\infty` and :math:`y_{2} \rightarrow +\infty` the expressions reduce to the two
dimensional result. Rather than rely on that limit numerically, a layer with **no** extents set is passed
straight to the two dimensional algorithms, so a model in which no layer has extents set reproduces the two
dimensional result exactly. A single model may freely mix layers of finite and infinite extent.

Setting the extent of a layer
-----------------------------

A layer is of infinite extent by default. To give a layer a finite extent, tick **Finite extent (2.5D)** in
the **Attributes** sidebar and set the **Y1 extent** and **Y2 extent** spinners, in :math:`km`. Unticking the
box returns the layer to two dimensional modelling.

Extents may also be set from the **Attribute Table**, using the **Y1 extent** and **Y2 extent** columns. An
**empty** cell in either column means the layer is of infinite extent. Both columns must be filled for the
extent to take effect.

To give every layer the same extent at once, or to return the whole model to 2D, use::

    Layers -> Set Out Of Plane Extent...

The along strike component of magnetisation
-------------------------------------------

For a two dimensional body the component of the magnetisation vector along strike, :math:`J_{y}`, integrates
to zero and contributes nothing to the anomaly. This is why the two dimensional formulation in Section
:ref:`A2 <magnetic_algorithm>` carries only :math:`J_{x}` and :math:`J_{z}`. Once the body has ends, however,
:math:`J_{y}` produces a real anomaly, and so it is carried here:

.. math:: J_{x} = J \cos A \cos(C-B)
    :label: rp_mag_J_x

.. math:: J_{y} = J \cos A \sin(C-B)
    :label: rp_mag_J_y

.. math:: J_{z} = J \sin A
    :label: rp_mag_J_z

where the angles **A**, **B** and **C** are as defined in Section :ref:`A2 <magnetic_algorithm>`, and the
anomaly is projected onto the inducing field direction as described there. Note that
this means a layer whose magnetisation vector is oblique to the model profile (i.e. :math:`C \neq B`) will
show a change in its calculated magnetic anomaly when given a finite extent, over and above the reduction
caused by the loss of the body's tails.

.. warning::

    There is no 2.75D vertical gravity gradient algorithm. Rasmussen and Pedersen (1979)
    :cite:`rasmussen1979` give the gravity and the magnetic field but not the vertical gradient, and gmg
    continues to calculate the VGG in two dimensions using the method of Section
    :ref:`A1 <manual_gravity_algorithm>`. **In a model containing any layer of finite extent, the gravity
    and magnetic anomalies will honour that extent but the VGG will not.** When this is the case the VGG
    axis is labelled *2D only* as a reminder.

Method testing
--------------

The 2.75D algorithms are covered by the packaged test suite, which may be run with::

    pytest --pyargs gmgpy.tests

The tests check, for both gravity and magnetics:

1. That a body of very large extent reproduces the two dimensional algorithms, and that a layer with no
   extents set reproduces them exactly.
2. That a body of finite extent agrees with an exact three dimensional solution for the same body. Because a
   rectangle is described exactly by four polygon nodes, the benchmark body is a right rectangular prism,
   which carries no discretisation error of its own. Gravity is compared against Nagy (1966)
   :cite:`nagy1966` and magnetics against a direct summation of point dipoles.
3. That splitting a body along strike gives the same anomaly as the whole body, that two layers modelled
   together give the same anomaly as the sum of the two modelled alone whatever order they are given in, and
   that shortening a body reduces its anomaly monotonically.

The benchmark extents are deliberately asymmetric, since a body that is symmetric about the section cannot
detect an error in the sign of the along strike direction.
